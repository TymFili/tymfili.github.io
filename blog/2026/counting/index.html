What Does it Mean to Count?

Created on September 12, 2026

2026   ·   set-theory   discrete-mathematics   logic   ·   mathematics

Cardinality answers the fundamental question: “When do two sets have the same size?”

For finite collections, we count by assigning labels: \(1, 2, 3, \dots, n\). For infinite collections, sequential counting fails. To compare infinite sizes, we need a structural definition.

1. Equinumerosity and the Pre-order of Sizes

The Hat Principle: Suppose seven people enter a room. Instead of counting on your fingers, you place one hat on each person’s head. If every person wears exactly one hat and no hats remain, you know the number of hats equals the number of people, without ever counting in sequence.

  • Two sets \(A\) and \(B\) are equinumerous (\(A \sim B\)) if there exists a bijection \(f \colon A \longrightarrow B\). Equinumerosity is an equivalence relation.
  • We say \(A\) is no larger than \(B\) (\(A \le_c B\)) if there exists an injection \(f \colon A \longrightarrow B\).

Hume’s Principle

Just as the Axiom of Extensionality formalises Leibniz’s Law of the Identity of Indiscernibles, Cantor’s definition of equinumerosity formalises Hume’s Principle: two collections have the same cardinal number if and only if their elements can be placed in one-to-one correspondence. In A Treatise of Human Nature (1739), David Hume observed:

“When two numbers are so combined as that the one has always an unit answering to every unit of the other, we pronounce them equal.”

Gottlob Frege later adopted this as the conceptual cornerstone from which all of Peano arithmetic could be derived. By replacing sequential counting with bijective pairing, Cantor freed cardinality from the constraints of finiteness.

2. The Schröder–Bernstein Theorem

Reflexivity (\(A \le_c A\)) and transitivity (\(A \le_c B \land B \le_c C \rightarrow A \le_c C\)) of the injection pre-order are immediate. However, antisymmetry is deceptively difficult: \(A \le_c B \land B \le_c A \implies A \sim B\) If you have an injection from \(A\) into \(B\) and an injection from \(B\) into \(A\), can you construct a genuine bijection?

Theorem (Schröder–Bernstein, 1897): Let \(A\) and \(B\) be sets. If there exist injections \(f \colon A \longrightarrow B\) and \(g \colon B \longrightarrow A\), then there exists a bijection \(h \colon A \longrightarrow B\).

Historical Note: Stated by Cantor in 1887 without proof, and proved by Dedekind in 1887 (unpublished). Felix Bernstein provided the first correct proof in 1897 independent of the Axiom of Choice. Ernst Schröder published a flawed proof in 1898, had its errors exposed by Alwin Korselt in 1902, confirmed the mistake, and died shortly after.

Proof (König, 1906) (click to reveal)

Case 1: Assume \(B \subseteq A\).

Here, the inclusion map \(\iota \colon B \hookrightarrow A\) provides the second injection. We only need to find a bijection using our given injection \(f \colon A \longrightarrow B\).

The elements in \(A \setminus B\) have no immediate home in \(B\). We resolve this by letting \(f\) push them forward, creating a cascade of displaced subsets: \(A_0 := A \setminus B, \qquad A_{n+1} := f(A_n), \qquad C := \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} A_n\) Define the function \(h \colon A \longrightarrow B\) by: \(h(x) := \begin{cases} f(x) & \text{if } x \in C, \\ x & \text{if } x \notin C. \end{cases}\)

  1. Well-defined codomain (\(h(A) \subseteq B\)):
    If \(x \in C\), then \(h(x) = f(x) \in B\). If \(x \notin C\), then \(x \notin A_0 = A \setminus B\), which means \(x \in B\). Thus \(h(x) = x \in B\).
  2. Injectivity:
    Suppose \(h(x) = h(y)\).
    • If \(x, y \in C\), injectivity of \(f\) gives \(f(x) = f(y) \implies x = y\).
    • If \(x, y \notin C\), then \(x = y\).
    • If \(x \in C\) and \(y \notin C\), then \(x \in A_k\) for some \(k\), so \(h(x) = f(x) \in A_{k+1} \subseteq C\). But \(h(y) = y \notin C\), so \(h(x) \ne h(y)\).
  3. Surjectivity:
    Let \(y \in B\).
    • If \(y \in C\), since \(y \in B\), \(y \notin A_0\). Hence \(y \in A_{k+1} = f(A_k)\) for some \(k \ge 0\). Thus \(y = f(x)\) for some \(x \in A_k \subseteq C\), meaning \(h(x) = f(x) = y\).
    • If \(y \notin C\), then \(h(y) = y\).

Hence, \(h \colon A \longrightarrow B\) is a bijection.

Case 2: General Case (\(B \not\subseteq A\)).

We have injections \(f \colon A \longrightarrow B\) and \(g \colon B \longrightarrow A\).

Consider the image \(g(B) \subseteq A\). Because \(g\) is injective, it is a bijection onto its range: \(g \colon B \longrightarrow g(B) \quad \text{with inverse} \quad g^{-1} \colon g(B) \longrightarrow B\) Now consider the set \(A\) and its subset \(g(B) \subseteq A\). The composition: \(g \circ f \colon A \longrightarrow g(B)\) is an injection from \(A\) into its subset \(g(B)\).

Applying Case 1 to the pair \((A, g(B))\) with the injection \(g \circ f\), there exists a bijection: \(h \colon A \longrightarrow g(B)\) Composing this with \(g^{-1}\) yields our desired bijection: \(g^{-1} \circ h \colon A \longrightarrow B\)

$$\boldsymbol{Q.E.D.}$$

3. What is a Cardinal Number? The Standard Ruler

Now that the Schröder–Bernstein Theorem guarantees that the pre-order \(\le_c\) is antisymmetric, equinumerosity classes are organised into an unambiguous hierarchy of magnitudes.

What, then, is a cardinal number?

The Problem with Classes

Frege and Russell originally defined a cardinal number as the equivalence class itself: the number \(2\) was taken to be the collection of all two-element sets. However, in axiomatic set theory, the collection of all two-element sets is a proper class—it is as large as the universe \(\mathbf{V}\) and cannot be treated as an object.

The Metric Solution: Standard Benchmarks

To assign a concrete mathematical object to each magnitude, we do what metrologists did with the metric system: rather than attempting to gather all one-metre-long objects, the International Bureau of Weights and Measures forged a single platinum-iridium bar in Paris and declared it to be the standard metre.

In set theory, we select a canonical benchmark set from each equinumerosity level:

  • \(0\) is represented by \(\emptyset\)
  • \(1\) is represented by \(\{\emptyset\}\)
  • \(n\) is represented by \(\{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}\)
  • \(\aleph_0\) is represented by \(\mathbb{N}\)
  • \(\beth_1\) is represented by \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\)

The cardinality of any arbitrary set \(X\), denoted \(\vert X \vert\), is simply the unique benchmark on our ruler to which \(X\) can be bijectively matched.

4. Cantor’s Theorem: The Scale of Infinities

Does this ruler ever terminate? Cantor proved that no set can ever be placed into surjective correspondence with its collection of subsets.

Theorem (Cantor): For any set \(A\), there exists no surjection \(f \colon A \longrightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)\).

Proof (click to reveal)

Suppose for contradiction that such a surjection exists. Define the diagonal set: \(B := \{x \in A \mid x \notin f(x)\}\) Since \(f\) is surjective, the subset \(B \in \mathcal{P}(A)\) must have a pre-image: \(B = f(a)\) for some \(a \in A\). Then: \(a \in B \iff a \notin f(a) \iff a \notin B\) which is an immediate contradiction. Hence no surjection exists, and: \(A \nsim \mathcal{P}(A)\)

$$\boldsymbol{Q.E.D.}$$

Because the map \(x \mapsto \{x\}\) is an injection \(A \hookrightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)\), Cantor’s theorem proves that the power set is strictly larger: \(A <_c \mathcal{P}(A)\) The measuring ruler extends infinitely into the transfinite: \(\vert A \vert < \vert \mathcal{P}(A) \vert < \vert \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(A)) \vert < \dots\)

5. Classifying the Infinities

A set \(A\) is countable if \(A\) is finite or \(A \sim \mathbb{N}\).

  1. \(\mathbb{N}\) is countable: Via the identity map \(\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{N}}\).
  2. \(\mathbb{Z}\) is countable: By interleaving positive and negative integers: \(f(0) = 0, \quad f(1) = 1, \quad f(2) = -1, \quad f(3) = 2, \quad f(4) = -2, \quad \dots\)
  3. \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable: Every rational has a unique irreducible form \(a/b\) with \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\). The map \(a/b \mapsto (a, b)\) injects \(\mathbb{Q}\) into \(\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{N} \sim \mathbb{N}\).
  4. \(\mathbb{R}\) has the exact cardinality of \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\):
Proof of $$\mathbb{R} \sim \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})$$ (click to reveal)

By the Schröder–Bernstein Theorem, it suffices to construct mutual injections between \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\) and \(\mathbb{R}\).

1. Injection \(f \colon \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}) \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}\):
For each subset \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\), define its image via a ternary expansion using only digits \(0\) and \(2\): \(f(S) := \sum_{n \in S} \frac{2}{3^{n+1}}\) Because expansions using only \(0\) and \(2\) avoid the dual-representation ambiguity (\(0.0111\dots = 0.1000\dots\)), distinct subsets map to distinct reals. Thus \(f\) is injective.

2. Injection \(g \colon \mathbb{R} \longrightarrow \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\):
Every real \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is uniquely characterised by its Dedekind cut of rationals strictly below it: \(D(x) := \{q \in \mathbb{Q} \mid q < x\}\) By density of \(\mathbb{Q}\), if \(x < y\), there exists \(r \in \mathbb{Q}\) with \(x < r < y\), so \(r \in D(y) \setminus D(x)\). Thus \(D \colon \mathbb{R} \longrightarrow \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Q})\) is injective. Since \(\mathbb{Q} \sim \mathbb{N}\), this induces an injection \(g \colon \mathbb{R} \longrightarrow \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\).

Conclusion:
By the Schröder–Bernstein Theorem, mutual injections imply: \(\mathbb{R} \sim \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\) Since \(\mathbb{N} \nsim \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\) by Cantor’s Theorem, it follows that \(\mathbb{R} \nsim \mathbb{N}\).

$$\boldsymbol{Q.E.D.}$$

6. The Naming of the Infinite: Alephs and Beths

How do mathematicians label the transfinite notches on our measuring ruler?

There are two distinct scales of transfinite cardinality, both indexed by the ordinal numbers (\(\alpha \in \mathbf{Ord}\)):

The Aleph Hierarchy (\(\aleph_\alpha\)): The Step-by-Step Ladder

The Alephs measure pure well-ordered size. Under the Axiom of Choice, every set can be well-ordered, meaning every cardinality appears somewhere on this ladder:

  • \(\aleph_0 := \vert \mathbb{N} \vert\): The smallest infinite cardinal.
  • \(\aleph_{\alpha + 1}\): The strictly smallest cardinal greater than \(\aleph_\alpha\).
  • \(\aleph_\lambda := \sup_{\beta < \lambda} \aleph_\beta\) for limit ordinals \(\lambda\) (such as \(\aleph_\omega = \bigcup_{n < \omega} \aleph_n\)).

The Alephs represent an exhaustive, step-by-step march through every possible well-orderable magnitude: \(\aleph_0, \quad \aleph_1, \quad \aleph_2, \quad \dots \quad \aleph_\omega, \quad \aleph_{\omega+1}, \quad \dots\)

The Beth Hierarchy (\(\beth_\alpha\)): The Exponential Leaps

The Beth numbers measure the explosive growth of power sets, starting from the natural numbers:

  • \(\beth_0 := \aleph_0\) (the integers, rationals, and algebraic numbers).
  • \(\beth_{\alpha + 1} := 2^{\beth_\alpha} = \vert \mathcal{P}(\beth_\alpha) \vert\).
  • \(\beth_\lambda := \sup_{\beta < \lambda} \beth_\beta\) for limit ordinals \(\lambda\).

Evaluating the first few stages:

  • \(\beth_0 = \aleph_0\): The countable discrete universe.
  • \(\beth_1 = 2^{\aleph_0} = \vert \mathbb{R} \vert\): The continuous line of real numbers.
  • \(\beth_2 = 2^{\beth_1} = \vert \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}) \vert\): The collection of all arbitrary subsets of the real line (or all functions \(\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\)).
  • \(\beth_3 = \vert \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})) \vert\), and so on.

The Continuum Problem: Where Do the Scales Collide?

By Cantor’s Theorem, we know that \(\beth_0 < \beth_1\). Because \(\aleph_1\) is by definition the immediate successor to \(\aleph_0\), the continuum must satisfy: \(\aleph_1 \le \beth_1\) Can there be any cardinal size strictly between the discrete natural numbers and the continuous real numbers? In other words: \(\aleph_1 \stackrel{?}{=} \beth_1\) This question is Cantor’s celebrated Continuum Hypothesis ($\mathrm{CH}$). The Generalized Continuum Hypothesis ($\mathrm{GCH}$) asserts that the two scales coincide at every transfinite step: \(\forall \alpha \in \mathbf{Ord} \ (\aleph_\alpha = \beth_\alpha)\) As Kurt Gödel (1940) and Paul Cohen (1963) famously demonstrated, the Continuum Hypothesis is independent of \(\mathrm{ZFC}\): it can neither be proved nor disproved from the standard axioms.

7. Exercises

1. Finite Binary Strings

Let \(\Sigma = \{0, 1\}\), and let \(\Sigma^*\) denote the set of all finite strings over \(\Sigma\) (including the empty string \(\varepsilon\)). Prove that \(\Sigma^* \sim \mathbb{N}\).

Solution (click to reveal)

Prepend the digit \(1\) to any string \(w \in \Sigma^*\), and interpret the result as a binary integer: \(f(w) := \operatorname{val}_2(1w) - 1\) Evaluating on strings in shortlex order:

  • \[f(\varepsilon) = 1_2 - 1 = 0\]
  • \[f(0) = 10_2 - 1 = 1\]
  • \[f(1) = 11_2 - 1 = 2\]
  • \[f(00) = 100_2 - 1 = 3\]
  • \[f(01) = 101_2 - 1 = 4\]

Since every positive integer has a unique binary representation starting with \(1\), subtracting \(1\) gives a bijection \(f \colon \Sigma^* \longrightarrow \mathbb{N}\).

$$\boldsymbol{Q.E.D.}$$

2. Hilbert’s Hotel: Absorbing an Element

Let \(A\) be an infinite set and let \(x \notin A\). Prove that \(A \cup \{x\} \sim A\).

Solution (click to reveal)

Since \(A\) is infinite, it contains a countably infinite subset \(S = \{s_0, s_1, s_2, \dots\} \subseteq A\).

Define \(f \colon A \cup \{x\} \longrightarrow A\) by shifting along \(S\): \(f(u) := \begin{cases} s_0 & \text{if } u = x, \\ s_{n+1} & \text{if } u = s_n \in S, \\ u & \text{if } u \in A \setminus S. \end{cases}\) The restriction to \(S \cup \{x\}\) is a bijection onto \(S\), and the restriction to \(A \setminus S\) is the identity. Since these form partitions of the domain and codomain, \(f\) is a bijection.

$$\boldsymbol{Q.E.D.}$$

3. Infinite Binary Sequences

Let \(\Sigma^\omega = \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}\) denote the set of all infinite sequences over \(\{0, 1\}\). Prove that \(\Sigma^\omega \sim \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\), and conclude that \(\Sigma^\omega\) is uncountable.

Solution (click to reveal)

Map each subset \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) to its characteristic sequence \(\chi_S \in \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}\): \(\chi_S(n) := \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n \in S, \\ 0 & \text{if } n \notin S. \end{cases}\) This mapping is a bijection:

  • Injective: If \(S \ne T\), pick \(n \in S \triangle T\); then \(\chi_S(n) \ne \chi_T(n)\).
  • Surjective: Any sequence \(s\) is the characteristic sequence of \(S = \{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid s(n) = 1\}\).

Hence \(\Sigma^\omega \sim \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\). By Cantor’s Theorem, \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}) \nsim \mathbb{N}\), so \(\Sigma^\omega\) is uncountable.

$$\boldsymbol{Q.E.D.}$$
